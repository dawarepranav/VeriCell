AI can outline every organoid in a microscope image in seconds. It also gets about one in five wrong, and never tells you.

[0:07–0:15]
This is VeriCell, built during the Meta AI Developer Hackathon on Muse Spark, SAM 3.1, Muse Voice and Muse Spark Open. Microscopy AI that knows when it's wrong.

[0:15–0:30]
A scientist asks a plain question, out loud if they like: which organoid is the largest?

[0:30–1:00]
Muse Spark reads the image and turns the question into a strict plan. SAM 3.1 and Cellpose-SAM outline 531 candidates, and SAM starts from words we measured it actually understands: the word 'organoid' alone finds nothing. Every candidate gets a calibrated chance of being real, and plain code measures everything. The model never invents a number. Then the agent decides what to double-check: Muse Spark judges two doubtful crops and hands them to the scientist.

[1:00–1:20]
The answer: 8,240 square pixels, with an honest range. But VeriCell also checks whether the conclusion itself could change. Organoid 321 is six percent bigger, and only fifty-nine percent likely to be real. If it is, it's the answer.

[1:20–1:35]
Say you want to publish: the largest organoid is at most 9,000. VeriCell stress-tests the claim. One organoid, thirty-six percent likely, would break it. This conclusion is one cell away from wrong.

[1:35–2:00]
So the scientist checks only the objects that matter. 321 is real: the answer moves, and the table shows exactly what changed. The other three are debris. Four clicks, and the conclusion holds. Test the claim again: robust.

[2:00–2:10]
Against the hand-measured truth, 8,818, the checked answer is within one percent. Before the checks it was seven percent off.

[2:10–2:16]
It can tell a microscope where to look again, and export every decision as evidence.

[2:16–2:45]
This isn't one lucky image. On a sixty-eight-image organoid benchmark, VeriCell beats the published agent on all sixteen question types. Its warning means something: when it says a size conclusion holds, it's wrong thirteen percent of the time; when it warns, forty-nine. It spots wrong cells better than SAM's own confidence, with a calibration error of 0.012. And the whole reasoning layer runs on open weights, Muse Spark Open, with matching results.

[2:45–3:00]
